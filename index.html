Master thesis, Utrecht University and NS, 2026

Worth the wait

How long will a train passenger wait for a quieter train, and what changes that answer.

Nederlandse Spoorwegen Mixed methods Python

Exploring passengers' willingness to avoid crowded trains

A crowded train arrives. You can board now and stand, or wait for a quieter one and lose time. The NS journey planner already shows a crowding indicator for every departure, but nobody had measured what that information is worth in minutes. I measured it, and turned the number into design rules for the app.

What I did

  • Phase 1Two focus groups with 12 passengers, transcribed and coded in NVivo, analysed with reflexive thematic analysis. Participants set the agenda for what to test.
  • Phase 2A discrete choice experiment built from those findings. 759 respondents, 9,108 binary choices, run in Qualtrics in Dutch and English through the NS panel and an open link.
  • AnalysisLogistic regression with generalised estimating equations in Python, stepwise model selection on QIC, confidence intervals by the delta method, and a mixed logit model as a robustness check.

What it changed

  • TimingCrowding advice works best before the passenger leaves home, where tolerance for waiting is about four minutes higher.
  • LimitsKeep suggested waits inside ten to fifteen minutes. At thirty minutes roughly three passengers in four refuse.
  • FramingEmphasise the size of the improvement, not the fact that a quieter train exists. Two levels quieter nearly doubles uptake.
  • PersonalisationLearn from behaviour rather than demographics, and only to withdraw a suggestion, never to push harder.

What I would do differently

Passengers said in the focus groups that an acceptable wait scales with journey length, so I expected a long journey to raise the exchange rate. It did not. It raised the chance of waiting at all and left the trade off unchanged. The gap between what people say and what their choices reveal is the part of this project I found most useful, and it is why I would not ship a rule based on interviews alone.

Master thesis, Utrecht University and NS

Worth the wait

The problem

The NS journey planner shows how crowded each departure is expected to be. That tells a passenger what is happening, but it does not tell them what to do. NS wanted to know whether the app could go further and suggest a later, quieter train. To do that responsibly you need one number nobody had measured for Dutch rail under normal conditions. How many extra minutes is one level of crowding worth.

My approach

  • Two focus groups with 12 passengers, using critical incident reflection, a board and wait argument exercise and a ranking round. Transcripts and reflection sheets were coded in NVivo into eight themes.
  • Those themes decided what the experiment tested. Passengers raised journey length, station facilities, weather and whether they were at home or already on the platform, so those became the four context attributes.
  • A discrete choice experiment in Qualtrics, in Dutch and English, using a resolution four half fraction design across the four context attributes and twelve within subject choice pairs.
  • 759 completed responses and 9,108 choices, recruited through the NS panel and an open link so the sample covered both older regular customers and younger travellers.
  • Analysis in Python with generalised estimating equations, stepwise selection on QIC, delta method confidence intervals, and a mixed logit model to check that individual variation did not undermine the population level reading.

What I found

  • Passengers trade about 10.3 minutes for one level less crowding when they decide on the platform, and about 14.2 minutes when they decide at home. The confidence intervals do not overlap.
  • Willingness falls steeply with time. 58.8 percent waited for a ten minute gap, 23.7 percent for a thirty minute gap.
  • Doubling the improvement nearly doubles uptake. 32.3 percent waited for one level, 59.3 percent for two.
  • Station size and journey length change how likely someone is to wait at all, but not the rate at which minutes are traded for comfort. Temperature showed no clear effect.
  • Trust in the indicator came first for participants. If passengers do not believe the prediction, the trade off never gets made.

What I recommended

  • Offer crowding advice during planning rather than on the platform, where tolerance is roughly four minutes higher.
  • Cap suggested waits at ten to fifteen minutes. Keep showing the indicator for every departure so passengers can still decide for themselves.
  • Scale how prominent a suggestion is to the size of the improvement, and describe it as a likelihood rather than a promise of a seat.
  • Personalise from behaviour rather than demographics, and only to stop showing suggestions to people who keep dismissing them.

Reflection

Situation

NS shows crowding information but had no evidence on what passengers would actually give up to act on it.

Task

Measure the trade off between waiting time and crowding, and find out which situations change it.

Action

Designed a two phase study so the experiment tested what passengers raised themselves, then built and ran the full analysis pipeline in Python.

Result

A clear exchange rate of ten to fourteen minutes, one significant contextual moderator, and a set of product rules NS can implement without new infrastructure.

Learning

The proportionality rule participants described in the focus groups did not appear in their choices. That taught me to treat interview findings as hypotheses rather than conclusions, and it is the main reason I now push for a quantitative check before a recommendation goes into a product.

Bachelor thesis, University of Cyprus

WebWise Academy

The problem

Most accessibility training teaches developers to pass a checklist. It rarely shows them what a website feels like for someone with a visual impairment, which is why compliant sites are still often unusable.

What I built

  • Three linked courses running from visual impairments, to WCAG standards, to assistive technologies
  • A colour blindness simulator and a contrast checker so developers experience the problem before learning the rule
  • Interactive exercises built with H5P, progress tracking and completion certificates
  • An OpenAI integration that answers accessibility questions in the context of the learner's own organisation

Outcome

Evaluated positively by supervisors and by pilot users from a developer community. The colour blindness simulation was named the most useful part of the course, which supported the argument that empathy tools work better than rules alone.

Demo

Reflection

Situation

A gap between accessibility compliance and real usability, with no training that closed it.

Task

Sole designer and developer for the full project, from literature review to a working platform.

Action

Structured the curriculum, built the platform on Moodle, wrote the simulation tools and made sure the course itself met the standards it taught.

Result

A working platform and the start of accessibility as a permanent thread in how I design.

Learning

Building an accessible course about accessibility forced every decision to be tested twice. It is the clearest lesson I have had that constraints improve a design rather than limit it.

Computer vision, Python and OpenCV

Motion detection camera

What it does

A security camera application that watches a live or recorded video stream, detects movement, draws a box around whatever moved, saves a snapshot and raises an alert. Built to understand how a vision pipeline behaves in practice rather than in a tutorial.

How it works

  • Frame differencing against a rolling background, then blur and threshold to remove noise
  • Contour detection with an area filter so a passing shadow does not trigger the alert
  • Snapshot capture with timestamps, so events can be reviewed afterwards

What I took from it

Tuning the sensitivity threshold is a user experience decision rather than a technical one. Too strict and the camera misses a real event. Too loose and the person stops trusting the alerts, which is the same trust problem that appeared in my thesis.

Machine learning, Python and scikit learn

House price prediction

The task

Predict property prices in Bengaluru from a messy public dataset with inconsistent units, free text location fields and clear outliers.

Approach

  • Cleaning first. Standardised area units, parsed size fields and grouped rare locations into a single category
  • Feature engineering including price per square foot, used to remove listings that were not plausible
  • Compared several regression models with cross validation, then tuned the strongest one
  • Best result of 0.86 on the evaluation metric

What I took from it

Most of the gain came from cleaning and feature work rather than from choosing a more complex model. That matches how the thesis analysis went, where the design of the data mattered more than the estimator.

Symbolic AI, Prolog

Context aware food advisor

What it does

A reasoning system that recommends whether to cook at home, order in or go out, based on what is in the fridge, the weather, how many people are eating and how much time there is.

Why rules rather than learning

  • Every recommendation can be traced back to the facts and rules that produced it, so the system can explain itself
  • New constraints can be added as rules without retraining anything
  • It works with no historical data, which is the situation most real products start in

What I took from it

An explainable recommendation is easier to trust than an accurate one you cannot question. This shaped the personalisation recommendation in my thesis, where I argued a journey planner should be able to explain why it suggested a later train.

Festify

The idea

An event discovery app for live music. The design problem was not the feed, it was the sign up. People will not hand over their taste in music to an app that has not shown them anything yet.

Screens

Key decisions

  • Onboarding reveals value first and asks for preferences in small steps rather than one long form
  • Filtering by date, price and genre sits on the feed itself, so refining does not mean leaving the results
  • A feedback loop of likes and ratings so the recommendations visibly improve with use
