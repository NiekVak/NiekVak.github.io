About Me

Designing for
people.

I'm a Master's student in Human-Computer Interaction at Utrecht University, coming from a strong background in Computer Science with a minor in Business Administration. My work combines technical rigour, design thinking, and behavioural research — with a particular focus on accessibility and inclusive design.

My BSc thesis was a full-stack Learning Management System specifically designed to teach developers how to build accessible websites for visually impaired users — a project that fused my engineering background with my growing conviction that accessibility should be engineered in from the start, not patched in later.

My MSc thesis explores passengers' willingness to wait for a less crowded train — examining the reasons behind their choices and how long they are willing to wait. The goal is to better understand user preferences in order to support more personalised recommendations and improved communication of crowdedness in travel planners. This takes me into behavioural research, preference modelling, and the design of information systems that adapt to real human decision-making.

What drives me is the conviction that good HCI work requires both depth — in methods, in code, in research — and genuine empathy for the people using the system. I aim to bring both to every project.

Festify

A personalised music event discovery app with a trust-first onboarding flow and adaptive UI. Designed to balance usability, customisation, and user trust.

Reflection (STARR)

Situation

An HCI design course project requiring a fully prototyped mobile app, evaluated on usability rationale and design quality.

Task

I led the UX research phase and owned the onboarding flow, preference screens, and filter system design.

Action

Conducted user interviews about event discovery frustrations, built low-fi wireframes, iterated on peer feedback, and delivered a high-fidelity Figma prototype focused on reducing cognitive load during sign-up.

Result

Onboarding praised for progressive disclosure. Filter system validated as intuitive in informal usability sessions.

Reflection

I learned that trust is the hidden variable in personalisation: users hesitate to share preferences unless they can see the immediate benefit. This shifted how I think about onboarding — less as data collection, more as a trust contract. If I repeated this, I'd run structured usability tests earlier in the process rather than relying on peer review.

Key Features

  • Progressive onboarding collecting genre, artist, location, and event type preferences
  • Personalised home feed with filtering by date, price, and genre
  • Event detail cards with sharing, bookmarking, and ticket links
  • Feedback loop: like/dislike suggestions and 5-star event ratings
  • Accessibility option: brightness toggle and clear typographic hierarchy

WebWise Academy

My BSc thesis: an LMS teaching developers to build accessible websites for people with visual impairments — combining pedagogical design, WCAG engineering, and full-stack development on Moodle.

Reflection (STARR)

Situation

BSc thesis addressing a real gap: most accessibility training focuses on code compliance rather than understanding the lived experience of visually impaired users.

Task

As sole designer and developer, I owned the full lifecycle: literature review, content architecture, UI design, and Moodle implementation.

Action

Reviewed WCAG documentation and AT literature; structured three courses (impairments → standards → tools); integrated H5P interactivity; built a colour blindness simulator and contrast checker; added an OpenAI API integration for contextual guidance.

Result

Evaluated positively by supervisors and pilot users from a developer community. The colour blindness simulation was highlighted as the most impactful learning moment.

Reflection

Designing a course about accessibility while ensuring the course itself was accessible taught me the concept of dogfooding at its most meaningful level. WCAG guidelines are necessary but insufficient — genuine inclusion requires empathy tools, simulation, and iterative testing with disabled users. This project is the origin point of accessibility as a core thread in my professional identity.

Key Features

  • Three structured courses: Visual Impairments → WCAG Standards → Assistive Technologies
  • Colour blindness simulation and WCAG contrast checking tools
  • H5P interactive exercises throughout each module
  • OpenAI API integration for organisation-specific accessibility guidance
  • Progress tracking and course completion certificates

Skoupa — Meditation Lamp

An AI-powered tangible interface for pre-sleep stress relief. The lamp listens to the user's voice, analyses emotional state, and suggests a personalised meditation with matching ambient light.

Reflection (STARR)

Situation

Group project in INFOMITI — design and build a tangible interactive system addressing a real user need.

Task

I owned concept development, user research (diary studies on student evening routines), and paper prototype evaluation. I coordinated the iterative design handover between prototype stages.

Action

Ran diary studies and contextual inquiry to map student wind-down habits. Insights led us to voice interaction (low effort, eyes-free). Led low-fi prototype sessions and synthesised feedback that shaped the functional build (touch sensors, ambient LED, voice-triggered AI).

Result

Positive course evaluation. Evaluators noted ecological validity and clear research-to-design rationale. Test users described the voice interaction as "surprisingly natural."

Reflection

This was my first deep engagement with tangible interaction — designing beyond screens forced me to think about how material, form, and ambient feedback communicate meaning. Leading the research phase also taught me that the quality of insights is directly proportional to the quality of your questions. I grew significantly in facilitating an international, multi-disciplinary team under time pressure.

  • Nikolas V. — User research lead, concept development, prototype evaluation
  • Susan R., Akanksha P.R., Ritesh K., Shivani A., Abdelghaffar A.

Petopia

A high-fidelity mobile prototype for a pet-owner social platform — built around familiar social interaction patterns with a focus on usability heuristics and emotional design.

Reflection (STARR)

Situation

HCI course project requiring a high-fidelity mobile prototype with documented usability goals and design rationale.

Task

I was responsible for information architecture, the lost & found reporting flow, and heuristic evaluation mapping against Nielsen's 10 principles.

Action

Mapped user journeys for three personas, mirrored familiar social app navigation to reduce learning curve, and explicitly documented error states and recovery flows.

Result

Informal evaluations showed users completing core tasks (post lost pet, find shelter) within 90 seconds without guidance — validating the familiar-patterns strategy.

Reflection

Petopia taught me that usability is often borrowed — leveraging existing mental models is not a shortcut but a design strategy. Designing for emotionally charged contexts (a missing pet) also revealed how much microcopy and visual hierarchy matter when urgency and clarity compete. This deepened my appreciation for content strategy as a full UX discipline.

Background & Motivation

Crowded trains are a reality many passengers encounter daily — especially during delays and cancellations when platforms fill quickly. In these situations travellers face a recurring choice: board the next train even if it is crowded, or wait a few minutes for the following, less crowded service. This thesis investigates how people make that decision, what influences it, and how long they are willing to wait under different circumstances. The goal is to translate findings into recommendations for more proactive and personalised crowdedness communication in travel apps — specifically the NS (Dutch Railways) app.

Research Aims

  • Understand the factors that influence passengers' willingness to wait for a less crowded train
  • Quantify how long passengers are willing to wait across a range of realistic travel situations
  • Translate findings into design recommendations for personalised crowdedness communication in travel planners

Research Methods

  • Literature Review — mapping existing research on crowding, public transport behaviour, and passenger decision-making frameworks
  • Focus Group — identifying and validating realistic travel scenarios that capture the breadth of passenger situations
  • Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE) — a quantitative stated-preference method presenting passengers with structured trade-off scenarios to measure actual willingness-to-wait thresholds

Expected Outcomes & Impact

  • Empirical willingness-to-wait thresholds across different passenger profiles and journey contexts
  • Identification of key influencing factors: journey length, crowding severity, time of day, delay context, personal preferences
  • Design recommendations for improved crowdedness communication and personalised advice in travel planning apps
  • Contribution to the growing body of HCI research on adaptive, context-aware information systems in public transport

HCI Relevance

This thesis sits at the intersection of behavioural research, preference modelling, and human-centred design. Understanding real-world passenger decision-making enables the design of adaptive information systems that serve people better — reducing friction, building trust, and supporting informed choices in daily mobility.

Reflection on my choices

The Human-Computer Interaction Master's programme provided a broad foundation in user-centred design, psychology, research methods, and interactive technologies. Through the compulsory courses, I developed skills in both qualitative and quantitative research, gained a deeper understanding of human behaviour and cognition, and learned how to design and evaluate interactive systems.

My elective choices allowed me to explore topics that complemented these foundations. Adaptive Interactive Systems and Mobile Interaction introduced different approaches to creating personalised and context-aware user experiences. Information Visualisation strengthened my understanding of how data can be communicated effectively and meaningfully to users.

AI-Driven Content Generation provided insight into the capabilities and limitations of generative AI technologies, while Responsible ICT encouraged critical reflection on the ethical, social, and societal implications of technology design and deployment.

Overall, the combination of courses helped me develop a balanced perspective on HCI, combining research, design, technology, and responsible innovation. These experiences have shaped the way I approach the development of interactive systems and user experiences.
